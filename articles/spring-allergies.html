When spring allergies get in the way of class

Northwestern students describe a lengthy and intense allergy season, while experts explain what's in the air and how people can respond.

By Carter Chau · 812 words

First-year student Eric Zheng said he's giving up on going to classes at Northwestern University this quarter. Seasonal allergies have caused him to miss several lectures.

“It's so embarrassing. My eyes just puff up, and I can't go to class.” — Eric Zheng

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reports that 27.5% of adults in the U.S. suffered from a seasonal allergy in 2023. In recent years, allergy seasons have lasted much longer with more intense symptoms. Spring 2025 is no exception, and Northwestern students report that they have been experiencing a lengthy and intense allergy season this year.

Pollen

Professor Gordon Smith, a professor of plant biology and conservation program assistant, said a mix of small particles such as dust, dirt and pollen contribute to seasonal allergies. Courtney Teng, a nurse practitioner at NewYork-Presbyterian Weill Cornell Medical Center, said pollen is the main culprit.

“When a lot of plants are starting to blossom, they send out pollen in the air, and that's when people start breathing in all those allergens,” Teng said.

Smith said a common misconception is that flowers pollinated by animals cause human allergies. Instead, it is wind-pollinated plants that are responsible. For example, the goldenrod flower gets the blame for seasonal allergies caused by ragweed pollen, even though only the ragweed pollen is carried by wind into the air.

Symptoms

Teng said seasonal allergy symptoms include a runny nose, itchy and watery eyes and sneezing, and that people can become drowsy from experiencing them. First-year student Noah Carver described a stuffed-up nose, watery eyes, malaise and fatigue. Second-year Madeline Huang said her eyes itch whenever she is outside.

For Zheng, itchy eyes have been the worst condition. “My eyes start getting really itchy,” he said. “I was rubbing my eyes the other day and I felt like I almost tore my cornea.”

Allergies this year

According to the American Lung Association, the 2025 pollen season is approximately three weeks longer in the U.S. now than it was 50 years ago. Smith said an increase in the volume and distribution of pollinating plants has contributed to the intense allergy season.

Medicine

Teng said over-the-counter medicines such as Claritin and Allegra are safe for treating allergies, while warning against overusing Afrin decongestant nasal spray because symptoms can rebound after extended use.

Students described different approaches: Connie Gillum alternates between Allegra and Zyrtec, Huang uses eyedrops and prescription allergy drops, and Zheng prefers to let his symptoms heal on their own—even when it means missing class.
